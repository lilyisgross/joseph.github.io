Analyzing the Ecological Landscape of Audiovisual Media Archiving Professions

Overivew

Our interview with Mark Toscano, as well our own secondary research, indicates that the field of audiovisual archiving (including film archiving, preservation and restoration, and management of collections with audiovisual materials) is still in the midst of professionalization, and consolidating itself as a distinct area of research. While there is increased interest in and competition for audiovisual archiving roles, there are also an increasing number of opportunities available. Similarly, while job candidates may be expected to arrive at roles with prior training, there are a steadily increasing number of graduate degrees and certificate programs offering such preparation; MLIS programs play an important role in providing students access to audiovisual archiving roles, though there are still avenues outside of educational institutions which may lead to such positions.

The wide range of job tasks, technical skills, and levels of collaboration required for Mark’s job is emblematic of the variety of these roles, and the types of institutions and contexts in which a media archivist might find him, her, or themselves working. The overarching umbrella of “project management” describes how Mark coordinates with various stakeholders – artists, their estates, lab technicians, catalogers and other LIS professionals at the Academy – to see a preservation project through to completion. In a day-to-day way, this includes things like sourcing film elements, inspecting archival prints, and visiting film labs. Tasks might be different in other roles, however. For example, a library with an audiovisual collection (as opposed to a film archive) might not require a preservationist, but might still employ a cataloger to describe and arrange its collection.

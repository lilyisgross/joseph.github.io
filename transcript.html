Q:Do you consider your professional role of film preservation as to fall within the LIS professions like library information science? What aspects of the work overlap with LIS tasks like appraisal, arrangement, description, retrieval, etc.? And what is unique to your specific role or your institution?

A:That's a tough one. Partly because I can't claim any kind of expertise or informed knowledge about the typical, duties and responsibilities, are in an LIS position, but my feeling is that it shares some things and, other things it doesn't, which is probably what you all already know. Which is to say that on one hand, there's the all the conservation questions in a very general sense. Obviously, the technical stuff is of course different, but that's just details really. But more conceptually and overall, I think there are some differences, which have to do with the different relationship of access to media – the different way that copyright and ownership can play a role. That's a big thing, actually. And I sometimes find when I talk to folks that have a more specifically library background, including even some of my coworkers, there's some stuff that, on a basic level, I feel should be very clear with regard to how film archives should behave, that some of my library colleagues or library-background colleagues in the archive, seem kind of at odds with ideologically, and it's just a different vision for it that's informed by a different background. And so like, for example, in my case, I work primarily with independent artists, sometimes individual sole creators of their work. And so they're also the sole author and owner of their work. And that's a very intimate, kind of meaningful relationship. It's a bit different than even like a Richard Linklater movie where, yeah, there's a whole auteur aspect of it, but there's all these other people who worked on it. It's owned by a studio or a production company and, you know, all this kind of stuff. And when it comes to that, the ownership of an artist's work by that artist or by their family that has a lot of meaning and, it just follows different rules in terms of representation and ownership and access than any other kind of non-media collection might follow, especially one that's not based on duplication, like a painting, or one that's based explicitly on mass duplication, like a book. And so I mean, they just follow different rules in terms of oversight and ownership. I butt heads a little bit sometimes with people about that. But other than that, there's still a lot of common facets, of course, in terms of cataloging and what kind of information you're documenting and how that might inform future engagement with the work or its future conservation. I think the work that I do is also very particular to how I've developed my own way of doing it over the time I've been here. If you talk to other preservationists, even that work on the kind of stuff I work on, they probably do it differently. We all do it differently from each other. And in my case, I've found I've taken, over the years, a much more kind of active curatorial role, which is not always something that preservationists are doing. There's a couple of archives I can think of […] where there's a main preservationist who has absolutely no agency or creative involvement in the curatorial aspect of the process. They're just like overseeing the technical aspect, which to me, that's okay. That's fine. That's a very intense division of labor. That's very old school […] I don't like this kind of very intense division of labor, kind of siloed – to use the buzzword – aspect of it, because often, a more aesthetic or curatorial or intellectual relationship to the content can really inform how you're going to make technical choices. And knowing, for example, why a filmmaker did a certain thing which seems maybe atypical or even wrong, but it was actually quite purposeful. And the person doing the technical work needs to understand that stuff. And I think, for example, of cases where I would give a film to a lab if I didn't tell them what to do with it, they would just treat it like any other thing, and that might be the absolute wrong way to preserve a film, you know, to remove scratches that are intentional or fix color that's weird on purpose, that kind of thing.
